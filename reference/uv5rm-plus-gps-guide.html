Before you press PTT: licence and legal

Listening is fine anywhere in the radio's range. Transmitting on its ham bands (144–148 MHz and 420–450 MHz on the US version) requires an amateur radio licence in the US (Technician class or higher) and a similar licence in most countries. The radio's own manual states it is not legal for FRS, GMRS, MURS or PMR446 channels, even though it can be tuned there. Some units, the UV-5RM Pro especially, arrive able to transmit outside the ham bands and with FRS/GMRS channels already programmed; being able to doesn't make it legal. Weather (NOAA), airband and FM broadcast are receive-only.

UV-5RM Pro or UV-5RM Plus GPS? This guide covers both

Baofeng sells this radio under two names: UV-5RM Pro (the name on the badge under the keypad) and UV-5RM Plus GPS. They are the same radio family: the same FCC ID family (2AJGM-5RHPRO), the same 10-zone firmware, the same Baofeng programming software, and both identify themselves to a computer as a BF-5RH. The 5RH Pro is a close sibling too.

What varies between units is the firmware version and, sometimes, which frequencies the radio will transmit on (see transmit frequency range). If something on your radio doesn't match this guide, check MENU › Radio Info › Versions, and trust the radio.

Emergency quick start

For someone who has never used a radio and needs help now. and keep a copy with each radio.

When this applies

If a phone works, call or text 911 first. It is faster and they can find you.

Normally you need a licence to transmit on this radio. US rules make an exception when life or property is in immediate danger and no other way of communicating is available: then anyone may use whatever radio they have to call for help. That is the situation this page is for. It is not a loophole for everyday use, and anyone can listen at any time without a licence.

How a radio like this works

  • One person talks at a time. While you hold the talk button you cannot hear anyone. Say your piece, let go, listen.
  • Everyone on the channel hears you. There is no dialling a person and no privacy. You call out and whoever is listening answers.
  • It reaches a few miles, less in buildings and valleys. Radio waves travel in nearly straight lines. Getting outside and getting higher helps more than anything else.
  • A channel is a frequency. Both radios must be on the same one. The big number on the screen is the frequency, or the channel's name.
  • Nobody may be listening. Unlike a phone, there is no guarantee of an answer. Keep trying, on more than one channel.
  • Key names on this page: PTT is the big button on the left side. MENU is the key with the green symbol at the top left of the keypad, EXIT the one with the red symbol at the top right.

Calling for help, step by step

  1. Turn it on. Twist the knob on top clockwise until it clicks. Keep turning to about halfway for volume. Check the antenna is screwed on.
  2. Pick the channel.
    • If there is a label or card with the radio, use the channel it names: press ▲ or ▼ until that number or name shows.
    • If there is no plan: press VFO/MR until the screen shows VFO, then type 14652000. The screen should read 146.520, the frequency most radio operators listen to.
  3. Listen for ten seconds. If people are talking, wait for a gap.
  4. Hold the big button on the side (PTT). The light turns red. Wait one second. Hold the radio upright, about two inches from your mouth, and speak slowly in a normal voice:

    “Emergency, emergency. This is  your name . I need help. Can anyone hear me? Over.”

  5. Let go of the button and listen for thirty seconds. A green light means a signal is coming in.
  6. No answer? Repeat it. Try three times, then move to the next thing in “If nobody answers”.

When someone answers

Speak in plain words. You don't need radio language. Give this, in this order, and say “over” each time you finish:

  1. Where you are. Address, cross streets, trail name, landmarks. For coordinates, hold EXIT for two seconds and read the numbers off the screen (press EXIT again before talking).
  2. What happened. “A man has fallen and can't move his leg.”
  3. How many people and whether anyone is hurt.
  4. What you need. Ambulance, rescue, someone to phone 911 for you.
  5. Your name, and that you'll stay on this channel.

Then stay on that channel and answer their questions. If you didn't catch something, say “say again”. Don't change channel unless they ask you to. The person answering is probably a volunteer who will phone emergency services for you, so give them time.

If nobody answers

  1. Get outside and higher. A hilltop, an upper floor, the roof of a car. Hold the radio up with the antenna pointing at the sky.
  2. Try 446.000 the same way: 44600000.
  3. Try the saved channels. Press VFO/MR until a channel number shows, then step through with ▲. Channels marked as repeaters reach much further. Call on each one.
  4. Find where people are talking. Hold # for two seconds to scan. When it stops on voices, hold # again to stay there, wait for a pause, and make your call.
  5. Try again every few minutes, and in the first five minutes of each hour: by a common convention, that is when operators in remote areas listen on 146.520.
  6. Save the battery. Switch off between attempts if help may be hours away.

If the radio has a repeater channel

A repeater is a relay station on a hill or tower that carries your voice ten times further. If the label or channel list names one, it is often your best chance of reaching someone.

  1. Select that channel with ▲ ▼. It is already set up; you don't need to change anything.
  2. Hold PTT, wait a full second, then make your emergency call. Let go.
  3. Listen for a short hiss or a beep right after you let go. That means the repeater heard you and sent your call out. Silence means it didn't: move higher and try again, or try another channel.
  4. If people are already talking, wait for a gap and say “Break, break, emergency.” They will stop and answer you.
  5. Keep each transmission under a minute, then release and listen.

Getting information

Weather and official alerts: hold 0 for two seconds, then press ▲ until you hear a voice forecast. News: press the top side button (SK1) for ordinary FM radio, then MENU to search for stations. Press EXIT to go back to two-way radio. Listening uses very little battery.

Before you ever need this

A radio nobody has practised with is not much help. Anyone can practise the listening parts today: turning it on, changing channels, weather, scanning. To practise transmitting, get a licence; it is one multiple-choice exam (see getting licensed). The first animated walkthrough shows these same steps on the drawn radio.

Out of the box

What's in the box

Radio body, lithium-ion battery pack, antenna, belt clip, wrist strap, charger and manual. Bundles vary: some add an earpiece, a longer antenna, a speaker-mic or a programming cable.

1 · Antenna

Hold the antenna at its base and screw it clockwise onto the top connector until snug. Never transmit without an antenna attached; it can damage the transmitter.

2 · Battery

With the radio off, lay the battery on the back about 1–2 cm below the top edge, lined up with the rails, and slide it up until it clicks. To remove, press the release latch above the battery and slide it down.

3 · Belt clip and earpiece

The clip fixes with the two screws above the battery. The earpiece or speaker-mic plugs into the two-pin (Kenwood-style) jack under the rubber flap on the side; push it fully home or the radio may think PTT is pressed.

4 · Charging

  • USB-C: the port is on the battery itself. Turn the radio off, plug in, and the indicator shows charging. An empty battery takes about 4 hours.
  • Desk charger (if included): light blinks orange when ready, red while charging, green when full. Takes roughly 2–5 hours. If the radio is switched on in the cradle the light may never turn green.
  • Charge fully before first use. A new battery reaches full capacity after two or three full charge cycles.
  • Charge between 5 °C and 40 °C. Don't charge a wet, swollen or damaged battery, and don't leave it on the charger for days.
  • For storage, leave the battery partly charged and somewhere cool and dry.

5 · First power-on checklist

First, clear the factory channels. UV-5RM Pro units have been seen arriving with Zone 1, channels 1–30 already filled with FRS/GMRS channels: the 22 standard FRS/GMRS channels (462.5500–462.7250 and 467.5625–467.7125 MHz) plus 8 GMRS repeater channels. To check yours, step through Zone 1 with ▲ ▼ in channel mode and look for frequencies starting 462 or 467.
  • Why remove them: this radio isn't certified for FRS or GMRS, so transmitting on them isn't legal, and a ham licence doesn't cover them. The radio has no receive-only setting per channel, so any of them will transmit if PTT is pressed. And they occupy the slots your own channels should have.
  • How: in channel mode (press VFO/MR until a zone and channel number show), go to the channel, then MENU › Program Channel › CH_Delete, one channel at a time. CH_Delete isn't in the menu in frequency mode (see the Delete a channel walkthrough). It's much quicker in Baofeng's programming software (see Programming on a Mac).
  • Never empty a zone completely. The Zone menu won't switch to a zone with no channels in it. This order replaces the factory channels and leaves your two family channels in slots 1 and 2:
    1. Delete channels 2–30, leaving channel 1.
    2. In frequency mode, set up your family backup channel and save it with CH_Memory, which now offers slot 2.
    3. Back in channel mode, delete channel 1.
    4. Set up your family primary channel and save it: CH_Memory now offers slot 1.
  • Already emptied Zone 1? Set a side key to Zone: MENU › Radio Setting › Press SK2 (or another side key) › Zone. Press that key and type 1; it switches to an empty zone where the menu won't (confirmed on a UV-5RM Pro). Then, in frequency mode, save a channel into it with CH_Memory.
  • Worth keeping for listening: anyone may listen to FRS. Putting back just FRS 1 (462.5625) and FRS 3 (462.6125, used by many preparedness groups) in a LISTEN zone lets you hear walkie-talkie users nearby; the example channel plan includes them. Transmit there only in a genuine emergency.
  • If your family wants to talk to FRS radios routinely, the legal route is a GMRS licence ($35, no exam, covers your immediate family) and a GMRS-certified radio, not this one.

The two modes you must know

Frequency (VFO) mode is a tuning dial: type any frequency and go. Good for experimenting and for setting up a channel before saving it.

Channel (MR) mode is a list of saved memories with all their settings. This is what you use day to day. Channels live inside zones: up to 10 zones of 64 channels each.

Switch with VFO/MR. Hold VFO/MR to change how channels are displayed: number, frequency or name.

    How repeaters work

    The idea

    A handheld reaches a few miles because hills and buildings get in the way. A repeater is a radio station on a tower, tall building or mountain that listens on one frequency and instantly re-sends whatever it hears on another. Both of you only need to reach the repeater, not each other, so two handhelds on opposite sides of a hill can talk across a whole county.

    Everyone listens on the output and transmits on the input. Your radio switches between the two automatically each time you press and release PTT; that is all a “repeater channel” is.

    The three settings

    SettingWhat it isOn this radio
    FrequencyThe repeater's output: what you listen on. This is the number repeaters are known by.The channel's receive frequency
    Offset (shift)How far, and in which direction, your transmit frequency sits from the output. Usually 0.6 MHz on 2 m and 5 MHz on 70 cm. The direction (+ or −) depends on the repeater and the region.Program Channel › TX Frequency (output plus or minus the offset)
    ToneA low hum sent under your voice, too low to hear. The repeater ignores any signal without it, which stops interference from keying it up. It is a key, not privacy.Program Channel › TX CTCSS

    Get any one of the three wrong and the repeater simply doesn't respond. A few repeaters use a DCS code instead of a tone (Program Channel › TX DCS), and some in Europe want a short 1750 Hz burst.

    Reading a listing

    Directories and club pages write repeaters in a compact form:

    146.940  −  100.0  W1ABC  Mt Example  OPEN

    • 146.940: listen here.
    • −: transmit below it by the standard offset, so 146.940 − 0.600 = 146.340. A + means above.
    • 100.0: the tone to send, in Hz. Also written “PL 100.0” or “CTCSS 100.0”.
    • OPEN: any licensed operator may use it. CLOSED or PRIVATE means members only; stay off those except in a real emergency.

    To put it in the radio, follow the Program a repeater walkthrough. To pull in the ones near you, use Find repeaters near you.

    How to tell you're getting in

    Press PTT, say your call sign and “testing”, and release. Then listen:

    What you hear after releasingWhat it means
    A second or two of quiet carrier, often ending in a beep or a short “pssht”You got in. That is the repeater's “tail”: it stays on the air briefly after you stop. The beep is the courtesy tone, telling the next person it's their turn.
    A voice or Morse code announcing a call signThe repeater identifying itself, which it does every ten minutes or so while in use. Also proof you're in.
    Nothing at allIt didn't hear you: wrong tone, wrong offset or direction, too far away, or the repeater is off the air.
    • You can usually hear a repeater from further away than you can reach it. It has more power and a far better antenna than your handheld. Hearing it clearly doesn't mean it hears you; move higher or outside.
    • Pause before you speak. Hold PTT for a full second first; the repeater takes a moment to wake up, and your first word is lost otherwise.
    • If others say you're “scratchy” or “dropping out”, your signal into the repeater is weak. Stand still, hold the radio upright and higher, switch to high power.
    • Checking whether you need the repeater: a short press of ✱ turns on reverse (R), so you listen on the input. If you can hear the other person there, you're in direct range and could move to a simplex channel.

    Linked systems and nets

    • Linked repeaters: many repeaters are joined to others by radio links or the internet (you'll see names like IRLP, EchoLink or AllStar in listings). What you say may come out on dozens of repeaters across a state or further. Leave a longer pause, two or three seconds, between transmissions so the links can keep up, and keep in mind how far your words travel.
    • Nets: scheduled on-air meetings run by a “net control” operator who calls on stations in turn. If you tune in during one, listen; when net control asks for check-ins or visitors, give your call sign. Don't start a separate conversation on top of a net.
    • Emergency nets: during a disaster a repeater may be handling emergency traffic. Listen and follow net control's instructions. Transmit only if you have an emergency yourself or are asked to. If you do: “Break, emergency”, then wait to be acknowledged.
    • Time-out timers: most repeaters cut off any single transmission after about three minutes. Keep each one short and let the repeater drop between them.

    Repeaters depend on power, a working site and a volunteer owner. They are the best tool for distance and the first thing to fail or fill up in a regional disaster, so a family plan should work on simplex first and treat the repeater as the extension. Etiquette for day-to-day use is under On-air basics.

    Reaching a distant area

    A handheld reaches a repeater 20–60 miles away at best. To talk to someone in a town hundreds of miles off, you need repeaters that are linked to other repeaters, so what goes into one comes out of the other.

    1 · Internet-linked repeaters

    Many repeaters have an IRLP, EchoLink or AllStar node. From a local repeater with a node you can connect to another node anywhere by dialling its number: hold PTT and press the digits, which the radio sends as DTMF tones.

    • Find a linked repeater near you and one near the destination on the same network: IRLP to IRLP, AllStar to AllStar, EchoLink to EchoLink.
    • Most owners only let members or control operators bring up a link, and many use an access code before the node number. Ask the owner or on the air first.
    • With a licence you can skip your radio: the EchoLink app on a phone or computer connects straight to any EchoLink node.

    2 · Wide-area linked systems

    Some networks join dozens of repeaters by radio links, so one transmission is heard on all of them with no dialling. The WIN System in California is one example. If both ends can reach a repeater on the same system, you can talk.

    Listings mention these as “linked to …” or name the system. Check the system's own website for which sites are currently linked.

    3 · Ask, or relay

    On a local net, “can anyone link to the Fresno area?” is a normal question, and the people who answer know which links work today.

    In an emergency you rarely need the distance: call locally, and ask whoever answers to relay your message or phone someone in the distant town for you.

    How the links actually connect

    Two repeaters far apart can't hear each other: hills and the curve of the earth are in the way. They are joined in one of two ways, and a third option skips linking altogether.

    When the internet is down

    In a regional disaster, power and internet are often the first things to fail, and every internet-linked path in the link finder fails with them. Plan as if that will happen. What still works:

    • Simplex and nearby repeaters on backup power. Many club and emergency-service repeaters have batteries or generators. Find out which ones near you do, from the clubs, and put them first in your plan.
    • Radio-linked systems, as long as every site in the chain has backup power and the links in your direction are radio rather than internet.
    • Relaying through people. Someone at the edge of your area can pass your message to someone further on, station to station. This is what emergency nets do.
    • Emergency nets. ARES and RACES groups (amateur emergency volunteers working with local government) run nets during disasters and pass written messages, sometimes hop by hop across the state. Listen for net control on local repeaters and ask them to send a message to the distant town.
    • HF radio. The proper tool for hundreds of miles with no infrastructure, bouncing signals off the upper atmosphere. It needs a different radio and a General class licence, one step above Technician. Emergency groups also use Winlink, email sent over HF radio, for exactly this.

    What this means for a family plan: a handheld like this one reliably covers your local area. For someone in another region, agree ahead of time on an out-of-area phone or text contact (calls between regions often get through when local calls don't), and treat a radio link to them as a bonus. Expect a message to arrive relayed and written, not as a live conversation.

    Finding a repeater's owner

    The owner, or “trustee”, is usually whoever holds the call sign the repeater identifies with, often a radio club. To find them:

    1. The node's information page names the owner: IRLP nodes at irlp.net, AllStar nodes at stats.allstarlink.org, EchoLink nodes in the EchoLink app. The link finder and your channel plan link straight to these.
    2. The repeater's RepeaterBook page often lists the sponsor, trustee, club website and access notes.
    3. Look up the call sign on QRZ.com (free account; often shows an email or club website) or the FCC licence search. A club call sign leads to the club's website, which usually has a repeater page.
    4. Ask on the air, or during the club's net. Regulars know who runs it and how linking works there.

    Expect to join the club. Many clubs share link access codes only with members. Dues are usually small and keep the repeater running. Record what you learn in the repeater's details in your channel plan: the i button on its row.

    On-air basics

    Making a call (radio to radio)

    1. Listen first for 10–20 seconds. If the frequency is busy, wait.
    2. Hold PTT, pause half a second, then: “W1ABC, this is K2XYZ.” Their call sign first, yours second.
    3. Release and listen. No answer after two or three tries: “K2XYZ clear.”
    4. To call anyone at all: “K2XYZ, monitoring” or “CQ, CQ, this is K2XYZ.”
    5. Keep each transmission short and leave a second of silence before replying so others can break in.

    Using a repeater

    New to repeaters? Start with How repeaters work.

    • Listen for a while before your first transmission to learn how that repeater's users talk.
    • To announce yourself: “K2XYZ, listening.” Don't call CQ on a repeater.
    • After the other station stops, wait for the courtesy beep (or a second) before keying. That resets the repeater's timer and leaves room for emergencies.
    • Most repeaters cut you off after about 3 minutes of continuous talking.
    • To join a conversation, say your call sign between transmissions. Say “break” only for something urgent.
    • If you can hear each other directly, move to a simplex frequency and free the repeater.

    Identifying (US rules)

    Give your call sign at least every 10 minutes during a contact and at the end of it. English letters and numbers, or the phonetic alphabet. You don't need to say the other person's call sign each time, and you don't need to identify when only listening. No music, no business use, no codes meant to hide meaning, and no transmitting without identifying.

    If you need help

    On a repeater or 146.520: “Break, break, emergency, this is K2XYZ.” Then give where you are (read the coordinates from GPS mode), what happened, and what you need. Stay on that frequency. If a phone works, call 911 first. US rules allow any station to use whatever radio means it has to call for help when life or property is in immediate danger and normal communication isn't available; that is the one situation where transmitting without a licence is permitted.

    Getting licensed

    • Technician licence (US): one 35-question multiple-choice exam, 26 correct to pass, no Morse code. Every question comes from a published pool, so practice tests show you the real questions.
    • Cost: a small exam-session fee (about $15 with most groups, some free) plus the FCC's $35 application fee. The licence lasts 10 years.
    • How: study with a free app or site (HamStudy is the common one), then sit the exam in person with a local club or online by video. Most people need 10–20 hours of study.
    • Each person who transmits needs their own licence. A licensed operator can let someone else talk while they stay in control at the radio.
    • GMRS is the no-exam alternative ($35, covers your family, 10 years), but it requires a GMRS-certified radio, which this one is not.

    Set up your set of radios

    Make them all talk to each other

    Two radios hear each other only when these match on both:

    • Frequency (and offset, if using a repeater).
    • Tone: if one radio has RX CTCSS/DCS set, the other must transmit that same tone. Simplest: leave tones OFF on all of them, or set the same TX and RX tone on all.
    • Bandwidth: Wide on all, or Narrow on all.

    Save the shared frequency to the same channel number in the same zone on every radio and give it the same name, so “go to channel 2” means the same thing for everyone.

    For GPS sharing, each radio needs its own identity

    Position sharing rides on DTMF signalling. Each radio needs a unique Radio ID (Radio Info › My Radio › Radio ID, or in the PC software) and the others listed as contacts/members. All radios must be on the same channel, with that channel on the main (highlighted) line, GNSS on, and Share Position / Request Position switched on. See the GPS sharing walkthrough.

    Fastest way: set up one, copy to the rest

    Program one radio fully, read it into the programming software (see Programming on a Mac), save the file, then write that file to each of the other radios. Afterwards change each radio's ID and name so they are unique. Without a cable, the one-touch search copies one frequency and tone at a time over the air.

    My channel plan

    What a channel plan is

    A channel plan is the list of channels you put into your radios, decided once and written down: which frequency goes in which zone and channel number, with what name and settings. With a plan, every radio in your group has the same channels in the same places, so “go to channel 2” means the same thing to everyone, and you can rebuild a radio after a reset.

    This page holds your plan. It saves in this browser as you type. Building one takes four steps:

    1. Choose your zones: what kind of channel goes in which zone.
    2. Collect your channels: the frequencies, from the other parts of this guide.
    3. Fill in the table, one row per channel.
    4. Put it in the radios, by keypad or computer, and copy it to every radio.

    Common mistakes

    • A receive tone on one radio only. That radio goes quiet to everyone not sending the tone. This plan only sets transmit tones; if you add receive tones, do it on every radio.
    • Wrong shift direction or offset on a repeater: it simply won't answer. Check the listing.
    • Saved into the wrong zone. The radio saves into whichever zone is showing. Switch zone first.
    • Deleting every channel in a zone. The menu then won't take you back to it. Keep one channel in the zone until your own is saved there; if it's too late, use a side key set to Zone.
    • Different names or channel numbers on different radios. The whole point is that they match.
    • Mixing wide and narrow between radios: one side sounds faint or distorted.
    • Leaving the factory channels. Units can arrive with 30 FRS/GMRS channels in Zone 1 that you mustn't transmit on, and they push your channels to 31 onwards. Clear them first.

    Saving and backing up

    • Save plan file downloads a backup of just the plan; Load plan file brings it back, on any computer.
    • Save to file and Autosave (top of the page) keep the plan together with the test log and labels. See your data and autosave.
    • Export CSV puts the repeater details (call sign, node, dialling, owner) in CHIRP's comment column, and leaves contact details and access codes out.
    • Print gives a paper copy to check radios against and keep with them.

    Range & accessories

    What range to expect

    VHF and UHF travel in nearly straight lines. Height and a clear path matter far more than watts. The “10 W long range” on the box changes little: going from 5 W to 10 W adds a barely noticeable amount of signal.

    Situation (handheld to handheld)Typical
    Inside buildings, dense citya few blocks to 1 mile (0.3–1.5 km)
    Suburbs, woods, rolling ground1–3 miles (1.5–5 km)
    Open flat country or over water3–6 miles (5–10 km)
    Hilltop to valley, clear view10–30 miles (15–50 km)
    Through a repeater on a tower or mountain20–60 miles (30–100 km)
    From inside a car, stock antennaroughly half of the above

    These are rough working figures; your terrain decides. VHF (2 m) carries a little further outdoors and over hills. UHF (70 cm) gets in and out of buildings and vehicles better.

    Line-of-sight calculator

    How far two antennas can “see” each other over flat ground or water.

     

    Two people standing on flat ground get about 7 miles at best. Put one of them on a 500 ft hill and it becomes about 35. Hills, buildings and trees in between reduce it.

    More range for free

    • Get higher: an upstairs window, a rise in the ground, the roof of the car.
    • Hold the radio upright with the antenna vertical and clear of your body; don't talk with it on your belt.
    • Step outside, or at least to a window facing the other station.
    • Take a few steps: at UHF, moving one metre can turn noise into a clear signal.
    • Use a repeater when direct contact fails.

    Field readiness

    Battery planning

    Baofeng quotes about 15 hours at 5% transmit, 5% receive, 90% standby. Treat that as a best case and plan on one full day of light use per charge, less with GPS on. I don't have measured figures for the GPS drain, so test yours once before relying on it.

    What drains it fastest
    • Transmitting on high power (by far the biggest)
    • GNSS left on
    • Screen always lit, high brightness
    • Scanning and dual watch (receiver never sleeps)
    • Cold: capacity drops noticeably below freezing
    How to stretch it
    • Program Channel › Trans Power › Low for nearby contacts
    • Radio Setting › Power Save › 1:2
    • BackLight 5 s, Brightness 2
    • GNSS off until you need a position
    • Agree check-in times and switch off between them
    • Carry it inside your jacket in the cold

    Reading the voltage (put Voltage on a side key, or set Power on Display to VOLTAGE). For a 7.4 V lithium-ion pack: about 8.4 V is full, 7.4 V is roughly half, and below about 6.8 V it is nearly empty. Readings sag while transmitting.

    Pre-trip checklist

    Agree a simple plan before you split up

    • Primary channel and a backup channel on the other band (one on 2 m, one on 70 cm).
    • Check-in times: for example the first five minutes of every hour. Radios can stay off in between.
    • If you miss two check-ins: what happens next (go to a named spot, head back to the vehicle).
    • A name for each radio so calls are clear: “Radio 2, this is Radio 1.” Licensed operators add their call signs.
    • Write it on the pocket cheat sheet so everyone carries the same plan.

    When you can't reach each other

    1. Check your own radio first: volume up, right zone and channel, the correct line highlighted (A/B), keypad not locked into the wrong channel, battery not flat.
    2. Hold SK2 (monitor) while they should be transmitting. If you hear them through the hiss, it's a tone or squelch mismatch, not range.
    3. Move: a few steps first, then to higher or more open ground. Hold the radio high and upright.
    4. Switch to high power for the call.
    5. At the next agreed time, try the backup channel.
    6. Try a repeater you both have programmed.
    7. If you had GPS sharing working earlier, your radio still shows their last received position in the member list.
    8. Follow the missed-check-in plan. Don't wander out of the area you agreed on.

    Emergency family channels

    Choose them by listening and testing from the places your family will actually be. A frequency that looks good on a list can be busy or unreachable where you live.

    First decide who will be transmitting

    On these radios, everyone who transmits needs a ham licence, including for practice drills. The only exception is a genuine emergency where life or property is in immediate danger and no other means is available. If some family members won't get licensed, give them FRS radios: your Baofengs can listen to FRS channels but can't legally talk on them. A plan you aren't allowed to practise is a plan that fails, so sort this out first. See getting licensed.

    The method

    1. Start from the simplex parts of the band, using your region's band plan. Nationally, 2 m FM simplex sits around 146.40–146.58 and 147.42–147.57 MHz, and 70 cm simplex around 445–447 MHz, but regional coordinators change the details. Don't make the calling frequencies (146.520, 446.000) your home channel, since everyone uses those.
    2. Load candidates and listen for a week. Use the button below, write them to the radio, and scan them at different times of day. Cross off any with regular traffic.
    3. Keep three channels:
      • Primary on 2 m: carries further outdoors and over hills.
      • Backup on 70 cm: better in and out of buildings and cars.
      • A local repeater, ideally one with backup power that the local emergency group uses, for when you are too far apart for direct contact.
    4. Test from the real locations: home, work, school, the usual routes. Mark where it fails; those spots need the repeater or an agreed meeting point.
    5. Set a transmit tone on every radio and leave receive tone off. You hear everything on the channel, and you can switch receive tone on later if chatter becomes a problem. A tone is not privacy: anyone can listen.
    6. Same slots on every radio: Zone 1, channels 1–3, same names, plus your strongest NOAA channel.
    7. Agree check-in windows, such as the first five minutes of every hour, so radios stay off in between and batteries last days.
    8. Practise monthly. Five minutes, everyone on the air from a different place.

    Programming on a Mac

    Status checked November 2026. Software support for the GPS version is still moving, so this section says plainly what works, what is experimental and what needs Windows.

    1 · The cable

    • You need a K-plug programming cable (two-pin Kenwood-style plug into the earpiece jack). The USB-C port on the battery only charges; it carries no data.
    • Buy a cable with a CH340 or FTDI chip. macOS has had drivers for both built in since 10.14 Mojave. Do not install any driver for them; old third-party CH340 drivers cause trouble on current macOS.
    • Avoid “PL2303” cables. Most cheap ones use counterfeit Prolific chips that the official Mac driver deliberately refuses. If yours is one, replacing the cable is quicker than fighting it.
    • On a Mac with only USB-C ports, use a plain USB-A to USB-C adapter or a hub that passes data.

    2 · Check the Mac sees it

    Plug the cable into the Mac (radio not needed yet), open Terminal and run:

    ls /dev/cu.*

    A working cable adds an entry such as /dev/cu.usbserial-1420 or /dev/cu.wchusbserial1420. Unplug and run it again: the entry should disappear. If nothing new appears, the cable (or adapter) is the problem, not the software. Always choose the cu. port in programming software, not tty.

    3 · Pick your route

    RouteRuns on MacState for the GPS versionBest for
    A · Keypad + one-touch copyNo computerFully worksA handful of channels; field changes
    B · Baofeng CPS in a Windows VMVia UTM, Parallels or VMware FusionOfficial tool; does everything including IDs, contacts and GPS optionsFull setup and cloning a set of radios
    C · CHIRP for macOSNativeExperimental: needs an unofficial driver module, uploads fail on some unitsPeople comfortable testing beta software

    Route B · Baofeng's CPS in a Windows virtual machine

    1. Install a virtual machine app (UTM is free; Parallels and VMware Fusion also work) and set up Windows 11. On Apple Silicon Macs this is Windows 11 for ARM, which runs ordinary Windows programs through its built-in emulation.
    2. Download Baofeng's CPS for the UV-5RM Plus GPS or 5RH Pro from baofengradio.com/pages/download inside the VM; owners report the 5RH Pro software reads the UV-5RM Pro too. Don't use the CPS for the standard UV-5RM Plus; it is a different program.
    3. Plug in the cable and pass the USB device through to the VM (in UTM: the USB icon in the window toolbar; in Parallels: Devices › USB). It then disappears from macOS and shows up in Windows as a COM port.
    4. Windows needs its own driver for the cable chip. On Apple Silicon it must be an ARM64 driver; WCH (CH340) and FTDI both publish one. Check Device Manager › Ports for the COM number.
    5. In the CPS: choose that COM port, Read from the radio, save the file as your backup, edit, then Write.

    A side benefit of the VM: CHIRP community members report that some copies of Baofeng's CPS and firmware updater are flagged by virus scanners. Keeping them inside a VM that has no access to your Mac's files is the cautious way to run them.

    Route C · CHIRP on macOS

    Where it stands: CHIRP's built-in “Baofeng UV-5RM Plus” entry is for the older, non-GPS firmware and does not match your radio. Support for the GPS / zone firmware (the same platform as the BF-5RH Pro) is being developed in CHIRP issue #11669 and GitHub pull request #1625. As of November 2026 neither is in an official build, and the CHIRP maintainer reported uploads failing on his own unit. A second driver written specifically for the UV-5RM Pro GPS (CHIRP issue #11834, pull request #1729) was being tested by owners in November 2026; it isn't in an official build either, but it is the one to watch for a Pro.

    If you want to try it anyway:

    1. Install CHIRP-next for macOS from chirpmyradio.com (pick the Apple Silicon or Intel build). First launch: right-click the app › Open, or allow it under System Settings › Privacy & Security.
    2. Check your firmware on the radio: MENU › Radio Info › Versions. The experimental driver was tested on software v2_0_09 / hardware v1_0_4.
    3. Download the latest driver module (.py file) attached to CHIRP issue #11669.
    4. In CHIRP: Help › tick Developer Mode, restart CHIRP, then File › Load Module and choose the .py file. It has to be reloaded each time CHIRP starts.
    5. Radio › Download from radio. Port: your /dev/cu.usbserial…. Vendor Baofeng, model BF-5RH-Pro (the name the module registers).
    6. Save that first download untouched as a backup before changing anything.
    7. Edit and upload. Rows 1–64 are Zone 1, 65–128 Zone 2, and so on.
    Known problems with the experimental driver: uploads that fail or loop on some radios; one report of zones being wiped after a bad write (fixed by restoring the backup); the per-channel speaker-mute option isn't represented; DTMF, contacts and GPS settings are unverified or absent. Keep the Windows CPS route available as your recovery path.

    Programming tips that save grief

    • Always read from the radio first and build on that file. Baofeng warns that writing a file that didn't start as a read can cause errors.
    • One backup per radio, named with its ID and the date, kept somewhere safe. It is your undo button.
    • Seat the plug hard; a half-seated K-plug is the most common cause of “radio did not respond”. Radio on, fully charged, on a quiet channel, nothing transmitting nearby.
    • Don't touch the radio or cable during a write. If a write fails, power-cycle the radio and write again before panicking.
    • Plan zones on paper first: for example Zone 1 your group's simplex channels, Zone 2 local repeaters, Zone 3 listen-only (marine, rail). 64 channels each.
    • Put your most-used channel at position 1 of Zone 1; that is what the radio lands on after a reset.
    • Cloning a set: finish one radio, read it, write that same file to the others, then change each one's Radio ID and name so GPS sharing can tell them apart.
    • Keep names to six characters or fewer so they display in full.
    • In CHIRP you can export to CSV and edit the channel list in Numbers, then import it back.
    • Leave firmware alone unless something is actually broken. Reports describe newer firmware built for a different circuit board revision causing keypad and backlight faults.

    Things only the PC software can set

    2-Tone and 5-Tone sequences, the contact list, the power-on picture (a 160×128 .bmp), and the full button assignments. The power-on password can also be read and cleared there.

    Unlock guides

    Unlock the keypad

    Hold ✱ (the key with the key symbol) for about two seconds. If AutoLock is on, press MENU then ✱. To stop it re-locking: MENU › Radio Setting › AutoLock › OFF. PTT and the side keys work even when locked.

    Remove or recover the power-on password

    You know it: MENU › Radio Setting › Password, delete every digit with EXIT, then MENU to save.

    You forgot it: connect the programming cable, read the radio with the CPS and clear the password field there, then write it back. Programming mode works without entering the password on the radio.

    Get out of Chinese menus

    MENU, 3 (third entry, Radio Setting), MENU, 8, MENU, choose English, MENU. Then the same with 7 for the voice.

    Transmit frequency range (“band unlock”)

    The US version receives 108–520 MHz in pieces but only transmits on 144–148 and 420–450 MHz. That limit is set in firmware.

    What I could and couldn't confirm for the GPS version:

    • Your unit may already be open. UV-5RM Pro units in particular are sold with transmit ranges far wider than the ham bands (retail listings claim 136–174, 220–260, 350–390 and 400–470 MHz) and with FRS/GMRS channels preloaded. A radio that will transmit on a frequency is not permission to: the legal rules below still apply.
    • No confirmed unlock exists for a radio that is limited to the ham bands. In the main community thread tracking this radio family, nobody has reported a successful GPS-model unlock, and the old UV-5R trick (holding keys at power-on until “Factory” appears) is not reported to work on it.
    • Check your radio's region: on related 5RM/5RH radios, holding 8 while switching on shows the lock state: “FCC” means US-limited, “BF” means open. Unverified on the GPS model, harmless to try.
    • Vendor “special” CPS: Baofeng and Radioddity have handed out modified CPS builds with a frequency-limit editor (one is named 5RM_CPS_special_ver_v1.2.0) to customers who ask support. Users report it working on non-GPS siblings and failing to connect on others. One user described the change as a one-way trip.
    • Firmware swaps that open up sibling models are reported as irreversible, because the original firmware isn't available to go back to, and wrong-board firmware has caused keypad and backlight faults.
    Before going further: an unlock doesn't change what you may legally transmit on. A ham licence covers the ham bands the radio already transmits on; GMRS, FRS, MURS, marine and business channels each require a radio certified for that service, which this one isn't. Airband transmit is not possible on this hardware at all. Given the brick risk and nothing gained legally, my recommendation is to leave the transmit limits alone and, if you need GMRS, buy a GMRS-certified handheld.

    Getting more to listen to (no unlock needed)

    Reception is already open across 108–136, 136–174, 220–260, 350–390 and 400–520 MHz. Type the frequency in VFO mode, or set a sweep with MENU › Scan › Freq Ranger. Saved listen-only channels work too: just don't press PTT on them, or set TX Admit so the radio blocks it on a busy channel.

    Save a channel

    VFO mode → type frequency → MENU 4 → set tone/power if needed → CH_Memory (17) → pick slot → MENU.

    Repeater

    Type the frequency you listen on. MENU 4 → TX Frequency = listen ± offset → TX CTCSS = tone → save with CH_Memory.

    GPS position

    Same channel on the highlighted line. Hold EXIT. On Host: brief PTT sends mine. On a member: brief PTT asks for theirs. EXIT before talking.

    Can't hear each other?

    Volume · zone + channel · highlighted line · keys locked? · hold SK2 · move higher · high power · backup channel.

    On the air

    Listen first. “Their call, this is my call.” Identify every 10 minutes and when finished. Emergency: “Break, break, emergency” + where, what, what you need.

      DCS codes

      Each code exists as normal (N) and inverted (I), for example D023N and D023I.

        DTMF

        Hold PTT and press number keys to send DTMF tones. The letter codes are on the top row of the keypad: MENU = A, ▲ = B, ▼ = C, EXIT = D.
